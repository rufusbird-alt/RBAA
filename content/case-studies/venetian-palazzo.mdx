A palazzo on the Grand Canal, a hundred years in one family, came to trustees who needed to understand what they had before they could decide what to sell, and what to hold for the beneficiaries. The collection - and the story - was the building as much as the objects in it: family portraits by a close family friend now categorised as national culture, furniture from the famous Music Room, correspondence and inscribed first editions.

The mandate was to consider the contents. Catalogue the collection. Understand the provenance. Value for eventual sale and consider the options. Organise off-site storage for what was to be held. Liaise with the real-estate agency handling the building itself. And, quietly, identify anything that had been mistaken for ordinary.

Two discoveries mattered most. The first was revealing : a cache of unpublished letters from Bernard Berenson, stored in a trunk, uncatalogued, this expanded the picture of the family in the twentieth century. The second was smaller but financially significant: a Vietnamese painter's portrait of a amily member hanging in the main hall, I sent it to Hong Kong for sale and sold for ten times its previous value.

The work took 18 months and is continuing. I have my own set of keys.

*The first job is always the same: know what is there. Everything improves after that.*

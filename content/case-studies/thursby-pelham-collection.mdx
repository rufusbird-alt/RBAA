## The Collection

The Thursby-Pelham family collection represents one of the most significant holdings of English furniture assembled in the 20th century. James Thursby-Pelham (1869–1947) was a celebrated collector whose pieces were used to illustrate the landmark first edition of *The Dictionary of English Furniture* by Macquoid and Edwards in 1924 — the foundational text of English furniture scholarship. His collection was featured extensively in *Country Life* and admired across the collecting community of R W Symonds's circle.

By descent, these pieces remained in the family for nearly 80 years. When the time came to bring the collection to market, the challenge was clear: how to position a large, multi-period holding in a way that reflected both its historical significance and current market values, without fragmenting it through selective cherry-picking.

## The Advisory Strategy

Rather than extract the highest-value pieces for separate sale — a common but destructive approach — I recommended an integrated dispersal strategy: research and authenticate overlooked pieces within the collection, use scholarly findings to build narrative appeal, and market the entire group as a coherent whole. This approach serves multiple objectives: it preserves provenance integrity, establishes the collection's institutional credibility, and paradoxically, increases the value of secondary pieces through association.

## Identifying Hidden Value

Within the larger group, I identified works that warranted deeper research. One painting — a portrait by Gillis Claeissens, the Bruges-based Fleming whose work was only attributed and distinguished as a named artist in 2017 — was catalogued historically under a different artist. I directed Sworders to undertake research into the work's attribution and provenance. Their research confirmed the Claeissens attribution and resolved the historical ownership chain.

This single attribution discovery had a strategic effect: it demonstrated the collection's scholarly depth and the quality of connoisseurship required to assemble it. It also signalled to the market that careful research would accompany the entire sale.

## Execution and Results

Sworders conducted the dispersal across a dedicated collection sale, with the research-authenticated pieces integrated throughout rather than isolated. The Claeissens portrait sold for £53,300 — a result that reflected both the artist's emerging market recognition and the confidence generated by scholarship.

More importantly, the overall collection realised significantly higher totals than would have been achieved through fragmentary sales or conservative estimates. By positioning the collection as a coherent group — complete with scholarly validation and documented provenance — each piece benefited from the strength of the whole.

## Why This Matters for Collectors

This transaction illustrates a principle central to collection management: sometimes the greatest value lies not in extracting individual stars, but in recognising and enhancing the coherence of the group itself. Families holding large collections often face the temptation to isolate and sell the "best" pieces first. This approach leaves residual material undervalued and provenance scattered.

A better strategy — one that requires genuine expertise to execute — is to understand the collection as a historical and market entity. Research overlooked attributions. Document provenance systematically. Present the collection with authority. This approach yields better financial results, preserves the collection's intellectual integrity, and positions each piece more persuasively in the market.

*For advisors and collectors alike, the Thursby-Pelham sale demonstrates that scholarship and strategy are inseparable from market success.*

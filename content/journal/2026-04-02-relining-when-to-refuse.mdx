The question of whether to reline a canvas is not purely technical. It is also, and perhaps principally, a question about how much risk you are prepared to accept in each direction.

There is the risk of the canvas as it stands: if the support is fragile, if the paint layer is lifting, if the stretcher is old and the tension uneven, then doing nothing is a gamble that the next environmental shock — a removal, a humidity change, a clumsy handler — will do damage that is not reversible. Against this, there is the risk of the intervention itself: relining is irreversible, the thermal and chemical procedures involved change the surface character of a painting permanently, and the history of conservation contains a significant number of works whose condition was not improved by the treatment.

## Three conservators, three readings

In the case before me — a seventeenth-century Flemish oil on canvas, 73 × 91 cm, from a private collection in <SmallCaps>Scotland</SmallCaps> — three conservators had been consulted. They agreed on the diagnosis: the original canvas was structurally compromised along the bottom third, a result of old water damage; the paint layer was stable but vulnerable at the junction between the damaged and undamaged areas. On everything else, they disagreed.

The first conservator recommended relining as soon as practical. The second recommended targeted consolidation of the paint layer with no intervention to the support, on the grounds that the canvas, though weakened, was not at immediate risk and that a lining wax would be visible in raking light. The third recommended a strip lining — reinforcing only the damaged area with a secondary canvas, leaving the rest of the original support intact.

All three positions were defensible. All three represented genuine professional judgements, not negligence or incompetence.

## The case for leaving well alone

Conservation orthodoxy has shifted considerably in the last thirty years. The doctrine of minimum intervention — do as little as possible, document everything, and leave options open for future conservators with better tools — now dominates serious practice. Relining as a routine preventive measure, which was standard in the mid-twentieth century, is now understood to have been responsible for the loss of a great deal of original paint surface.

The tone change after wax-resin lining is one of the most persistent problems: the surface becomes slightly dull, the micro-texture of the original ground is compressed, and the painting loses something — not always dramatically, but permanently. In a work of high quality, this matters.

## The reservations

None of this means that relining is always wrong. Where the canvas is genuinely failing — where the paint is about to detach in sheets, where there are structural tears or deep creases that cannot be addressed by less invasive means — the question is not whether to intervene but how. The three conservators' disagreement was not about whether the painting needed attention; it was about the appropriate level of that attention.

The decision, in the end, was the strip lining. Six months on, the painting is stable, the intervention is nearly invisible, and the original canvas has not been subjected to the heat and pressure of a full relining. Whether that decision will prove correct in thirty years is a question neither I nor the conservators can answer.

What I can say is that the process of consulting three conservators, comparing their assessments, and making a decision with the owner's full understanding of the options — this is the process. It is not quick, and it is not cheap, but the alternative is to make a permanent decision about a potentially irreplaceable object on the basis of one opinion or none.

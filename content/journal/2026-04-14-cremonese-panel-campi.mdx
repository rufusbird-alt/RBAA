A small devotional panel surfaced at a provincial French sale in February, catalogued as circle of <SmallCaps>Parmigianino</SmallCaps>. The drapery said otherwise.

The sale was in Clermont-Ferrand — not a significant market — and the estimate was €800 to €1,200, which is the estimate that gets assigned to things nobody has really looked at. The attribution to <SmallCaps>Parmigianino</SmallCaps>'s circle is one of several categories of sale-room hedging that serve as a flag to anyone who reads them carefully: it says, simultaneously, that the cataloguer saw something Italian, something sixteenth-century, and something they couldn't place with confidence. <SmallCaps>Parmigianino</SmallCaps> is a capacious umbrella. Many things shelter under it.

The drapery — specifically, the handling of the blue mantle on the Virgin figure — was the first thing that didn't fit. <SmallCaps>Parmigianino</SmallCaps>'s followers share a particular way of folding cloth: the breaks are abrupt, and the highlights are dry and almost calligraphic. What this panel showed instead was a softer modelling, with a particular buff-yellow tone in the half-shadows that I associated with a different Lombard tradition entirely. <SmallCaps>Cremona</SmallCaps>, specifically.

## The Campi problem

The Campi dynasty — <SmallCaps>Galeazzo</SmallCaps>, <SmallCaps>Giulio</SmallCaps>, <SmallCaps>Antonio</SmallCaps>, and <SmallCaps>Vincenzo</SmallCaps> — has been better served by scholarship in the last thirty years than in the previous three centuries, but the workshop question remains genuinely difficult. <SmallCaps>Giulio</SmallCaps> ran a productive studio from the 1530s onwards, and the distinction between an autograph work and a workshop piece executed under his supervision is often impossible to draw on visual evidence alone. The surviving documentation is thin, and the physical evidence — pigment analysis, dendrochronology where applicable, infrared reflectography — has been applied unevenly across the corpus.

What we have is a group of works with shared characteristics: a particular way of setting the figures against shallow architectural backgrounds, a handling of infant flesh that is warmer and softer than the <SmallCaps>Parma</SmallCaps> school, and a consistent palette in the shadows that runs from deep umber to the buff-yellow I mentioned above. This panel had all three.

## The research trail

The first step was to establish what the sale catalogue had not: a provenance before the Auvergne family. The 1947 and 1938 estate inventories described the work only as *tableau religieux, école italienne*, which tells us nothing. The work was not in any of the standard Campi literature — not in <SmallCaps>Gregori</SmallCaps>'s 1985 monograph, not in the 2004 <SmallCaps>Cremona</SmallCaps> exhibition catalogue, and not in the recent digital surveys of Lombard painting in French private collections.

That absence is itself a clue. Works that have been in provincial French collections since the nineteenth century often arrived via one of three routes: the post-Revolutionary dispersal of Italian ecclesiastical property, the collection-building of French industrialists with Italian business connections in the 1870s–1900s, or the particular trade in devotional panels that ran through <SmallCaps>Lyon</SmallCaps> and <SmallCaps>Grenoble</SmallCaps> in the mid-nineteenth century. Each route carries different implications for provenance and authenticity.

## Tentative conclusions

This is where the essay must be honest about its limits. I can argue, on stylistic grounds, that this panel is Cremonese, mid-sixteenth century, and consistent with the workshop of <SmallCaps>Giulio Campi</SmallCaps>. I cannot, without further technical analysis, determine whether <SmallCaps>Giulio</SmallCaps> himself touched the panel or whether it was produced entirely by workshop hands to his design.

What I can say is that the work is significantly undervalued at its estimate, that the attribution to <SmallCaps>Parmigianino</SmallCaps>'s circle is almost certainly wrong, and that the correct attribution — once established — would place it in a completely different commercial category. The research is ongoing. A note will follow when the technical analysis is complete.

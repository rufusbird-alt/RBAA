Depending on who you are and your point of view, collecting art is an extension of your worldview, a fashion statement, a way to telegraph one’s wealth or limitations in that direction, how clever or esoteric one might be. It might even occasionally be — if some asset managers are to be believed — some sort of investment.

The physical appeal — or beauty — of a work of art is only part of the rational and emotional response to parting with cash for an artwork. However, there is a category of artwork which seems to transcend narrow collecting areas, and may even buck market trends, sending art world experts back to their crystal balls wondering why such and such a work sold for a tremendous price, when all the trends or data or analysis and expertise from connoisseurs and specialists point in the opposite direction.

Leonardo’s *Salvator Mundi* might qualify, especially so as the picture was heavily restored prior to sale. Another example is Gainsborough’s *Blue Boy*, in the Huntington Library and Art Gallery in California, though bought for a huge price at the height of the market for British eighteenth century portraiture.

The Rothschilds were very good at buying spectacular works of art, often at outrageously high prices, usually in competition with other family members. Queen Marie-Antoinette’s writing-table by Jean-Henri Riesener is just such a piece: Baron Ferdinand de Rothschild paid the enormous sum of £6,000 at the Hamilton Palace sale at Christie’s in 1882. The *Times* reported the next day “If Juvenal had been present at the auction rooms in King-street yesterday, he would have seen the man who gave six thousand sesterces for a mullet outdone by another who gave six thousand pounds sterling for a writing-table”.

Such breathless writing captures precisely the mix of admiration and incredulity with which the press greeted the idea that a mere table could command such a price. The same goes for art which is sometimes deemed incomprehensible to many, and consequently becomes an elitist prize: the huge sums paid for the drip-paintings by the American artist Jackson Pollock, often elicit a similar reaction.

Whatever one thinks of the artwork or the prices paid, these are unquestionably trophies. The more public the battle to win one of these prized possessions, the more rewarding it is for the (very rich) owner.

trophy art must fulfill the following criteria: 1. rarity: either there are simply a tiny number of similar artworks by the same artist, or most surviving works are now out of reach in museums. 2. panache: it must look *punchy*. It will be a centrepiece and therefore cannot look pale and interesting. 3. quality: it must actually be a good work of art.

And that’s about it — any more criteria and the field narrows too much. Provenance matters to some but not all. The rule can be applied to just about any big ticket sale since the Battle of Waterloo, after which the international art market began to expand. The trend in buying dinosaur skeletons can be interpreted in this way.

In early July the remarkable sale of “The Hamilton Laocoön” (above), as Sotheby’s dubbed it, is a case in point. This was one of four life-size bronze casts made from the famous ancient Roman marble statue in the Vatican. It had rarity because whilst there are many other large and small versions in marble and plaster, few are in bronze.

Cleaned and given a sympathetic polish when on view in Sotheby’s London in late June it had panache. It was not the very best cast — one at Houghton Hall in Norfolk by Girardon is probably the best — but the Hamilton Laocoön was by the little-known bronze sculptor Auguste-Jean- Marie Carbonneaux and of good quality (*above*).

Sotheby’s estimated it at £2 to £3 million which many thought might be a bit much for a late cast of the Laocoön by an obscure sculptor. On the contrary: several private and institutional bidders competed for it and it sold for a record auction price of £13.6 million, apparently to a buyer who does not normally buy “old” sculpture.

at christie’s, two ravishing flower paintings by Dutch artist Jan van Huysum also fit the trophy description. They are high quality and packed a visual punch, although they are not especially large, they are larger than most Dutch flower paintings. They *dazzled*. Flower paintings by van Huysum are not that rare, but these two are probably the best. Both sold to the same buyer for double the low auction estimate (£6.5m and £5.5m)

Could it be that buyers of trophy art by Pollock, Klimt, Basquiat or Picasso are now seeking much more reasonable trophies from other parts of the art market?

The three categories — copy, replica, and workshop version — are treated as though they were interchangeable in the sale room. They are not interchangeable. The distinctions matter, and the money knows it, even when the cataloguing pretends otherwise.

A *copy* is made by someone other than the artist, after the original work, typically at some distance in time. It may be excellent or poor; it tells you about the original's reputation, about the skill of the copyist, about the conditions under which it was made. It tells you relatively little about the original artist's hand, and its commercial value reflects this: it is the record of a record, however accomplished.

A *replica* is a repetition of the composition, usually at the same scale, often made in the artist's workshop and sometimes with the artist's participation. The line between a fully autograph work and a workshop replica with retouching by the master is one of the most contested in attribution studies. The value of a replica depends almost entirely on the degree of the master's involvement, and that degree is often impossible to establish with certainty.

A *workshop version* sits between these: made in the studio, by assistants, using the cartoons and under the direction of the master. The degree of the master's involvement — none, some, extensive — is the question that determines value, and it is a question that cannot always be answered.

## Why the distinction matters commercially

Consider a hypothetical: three versions of the same composition by a significant Flemish seventeenth-century master. The first is autograph. The second is a workshop version, studio-executed, possibly with retouching by the master in the face and hands. The third is a copy made by a gifted contemporary follower.

At auction, the price differential between these three works can be enormous — a factor of ten, twenty, or more, depending on the artist. The workshop version, which looks nearly identical to the autograph and was made in the same room by people trained by the same master, may sell for a fraction of the autograph price. The copy, which may be equally beautiful and equally well-preserved, may be almost unsaleable at a meaningful figure.

This is not irrationality on the market's part. It is a correct reading of what the objects are. The autograph work is the record of a particular mind's encounter with a problem. The workshop version is the record of a system. The copy is the record of an admiration. All three are interesting. They are not the same thing.

## The cataloguing problem

The problem is that sale room cataloguing regularly conflates these categories, either through ignorance, through uncertainty, or through a commercial calculation that a vague attribution is better than a specific one that might be challenged. "Circle of," "follower of," "attributed to," "studio of," "workshop of" — these phrases have precise meanings in attribution theory, and they are used loosely in practice.

The collector who learns to read these phrases carefully — who understands that "studio of" implies a different set of possibilities from "workshop of," and that both differ from "attributed to" — is the collector who is best placed to find works that are undervalued relative to their quality, and to avoid works that are overvalued relative to what they actually are.

The distinctions are not arcane. They are the vocabulary of the subject. And the money, in the end, follows the vocabulary.

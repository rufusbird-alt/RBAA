There is a particular kind of Englishman, usually well-intentioned but misguided, who arrives in a foreign country dressed for a role that ended eighty years ago, and I had a strong feeling that in spite of my best efforts, I would end up being him. I had brought tweed breeks, a shirt and tie, and a waistcoat cut on a northern Pakistani pattern by an English tailor, which is either a compliment or a circular joke depending on your point of view. My host had invited me to Pakistan to escape the British winter in early February to shoot partridge and to look at his manuscripts. My natural reticence in examining Islamic manuscripts was overcome by my desire to pay my first visit to this fascinating country, and to take up the generous invitation and rare opportunity to witness truly wild grey partridge shooting.

Pir Muhammad Sadiq is Pir of Luari Sharif and of Qazi Ahmed, hereditary guardian of two Sufi shrines and spiritual leader to a great many people across Sindh. The two shrines link his family’s line, respectively, to Abu Bakr, the First Caliph, father-in-law of the Prophet Muhammed, and to Abd al-Mutalib, grandfather of the Prophet. He is also, it turns out, a man who keeps Fortnum’s cheddar in the fridge of a shooting lodge two hundred miles from anywhere.

## Karachi

Karachi traffic is not a system but a negotiation, conducted at speed and settled by nerve. Our driver’s technique at junctions - all drivers disregarded traffic lights - was to advance into the oncoming column without hesitation or apology, on the theory – correct, as it proved, every time – that a Toyota Land Cruiser moving with conviction will be given room. I sat in the back and thought about the phrase ‘defensive driving’, which had suddenly acquired the quaintness of a Victorian parlour game.

We stopped at the Garden House, the family’s former home near Karachi Zoo: a late nineteenth-century villa in European classical dress, with coloured glass set in round-headed windows, now used chiefly for storage and for meetings. Shoes off. A sofa. And then, unwrapped from their textile sheaths came the historic illuminated manuscripts, one after another, the Qur’ans – Arabic above, Urdu below, commentary in the margins, gold laid on with a confidence that has not been achieved by Europeans for centuries. Pir’s librarian watched me look at them with the particular patience of a man who has seen a good many visitors fail to know what they are looking at. I sympathised with him as I was not entirely sure if he approved of my being there.

Lunch was at the Boat Club, overlooking Chinna Creek, in the company of politicians, businessmen and the heads of two interfaith organisations, which is a combination that in England would take a fortnight to assemble and here took a telephone call.

## Qazi Ahmed

We drove north at eight the next morning. Somewhere past Nawab Wali Muhammad the car was suddenly full of rose petals, thrown through the open windows by people at the roadside who had come out to honour their Pir. This happened again at the shooting lodge, and again as we got out. I have been welcomed to a good many houses in my professional life, but never once by petal.

The ground was mustard, laid out in fields of about an acre. The guns – four of us – took position along one edge. Beyond, below an embankment, stood a long line of men in bright clothes holding poles with white streamers at the tip. A whistle went. The line began to advance, hollering and bellowing, and I stood there in my tweed with a borrowed twelve-bore, watching a great many local people walk directly towards me, and experienced a brief and instructive vision of what my forebears had made of this country.

The feeling passed. It was replaced by the more pressing problem of the partridge, which behaved as though it had been briefed. A bird burst out far to my left and departed unharmed. Another rose in front of me and flew too low to shoot. This pattern held, with variations, through something over twenty drives and most of a day. I have shot in England for years. I could not hit these birds.

At two o’clock we were led to a tent hung on three sides with printed cottons, where a table stood loaded with nuts, cake, fruit and orange juice, and the four guns were seated while local dignitaries came and went. Then back to the mustard until the sun went down, at which point my gun was taken from my hands with a finality I chose to interpret as courtesy.

Dinner was a goat, slow-cooked with rice packed into its belly, served with curries, roast partridge, chicken and salad on a low table before the four of us while a considerable crowd watched from the edge of the room. The goat’s head arrived on its own plate and the brain was scooped out for my benefit. It is excellent – rich, faintly sweet, closer to sweetbread than to anything else in the European cooking. What we left went to the village, which is the point of cooking that much.

Afterwards Pir took me to his house in the town, where we sat cross-legged on the floor and more manuscripts came out. A Qu’ran by Princess Zeb-un-Nissa (1638–1702), daughter of the Emperor Aurangzeb, who wrote under the name Makhfi, the Hidden One (a rare manuscript volume of her poetry is to be found in the Bodleian Library in Oxford). A jewelled cylinder, probably seventeenth-century Mughal, containing a filigree case, containing a single hair of the Prophet. A water jug pierced through the centre of its base which nonetheless holds water and pours it, by a trick of hydraulics I did not solve and was too proud to ask about.

We walked from there to the mausoleum of the Pirs of Qazi Ahmed, a building late eighteenth or early nineteenth century in date, the interior worked in geometric panels and Qur’anic inscriptions, the coffins under green and gold silk strewn with dried rosebuds. Coming out, we found what appeared to be the entire town drawn up in the street, children seated at the front, then the young, then their elders, waiting in silence for a sight of their spiritual leader. Pir waved. They let us pass.

We ended the day round a fire with cigars and Fortnum’s cheese.

## The Indus

The next shoot was on an island in the Indus, which floods for two months every summer and is therefore among the most fertile and least permanent landscapes on earth. We crossed at a shallow point, drove along dirt tracks, and arrived at a newly built lodge – a wall, a courtyard, some very basic rooms – shortly before sunset. Here we met our host, Mr Suhail Abbasi, whose family are of Abbasid descent, and he is a scion of the Kalhora Dynasty, rulers of Sindh under Mughal Empire before the Talpurs domination in these parts in the 19th century. They were once major landowners here up until the land reforms of Pakistan post-partition. Another of Pir’s guests, Mr Asif Sana had generously arranged for a chef to drive down from Peshawar and cook a particular lamb prized for the delicacy of its fat: chunks of the fat were rendered in a cauldron over charcoal, the meat tipped in, salted, covered, and left alone to cook slowly for two hours. It was the best thing I ate in Pakistan, and it had two ingredients.

I was assigned a small team of helpers under a head keeper of advanced years and improbable red hair, who spent the day gesticulating and arguing in Urdu with anyone within range. Late in the day we came to a farmyard of wattle and stick, half fallen in, and I found myself thinking of Rembrandt’s *Landscape with Cottages and a Haybarn* of 1641. This is what happens when you send a man who looks at pictures for a living to a chickpea field in Sindh.

We left for Badin at eight in the evening. The roads were solid. A police escort materialised, switched on its lights and siren, and led us through the traffic for the better part of an hour, which I record here without comment except to say that it did nothing whatever to correct my sense of having wandered into the wrong century. We reached Badin at half past two in the morning.

## Luari Sharif

<figure><img src="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0fecbc06-3c36-4c52-90fe-97c92ad60ddf_4080x3072.jpeg" srcset="https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_640,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0fecbc06-3c36-4c52-90fe-97c92ad60ddf_4080x3072.jpeg 640w, https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1080,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0fecbc06-3c36-4c52-90fe-97c92ad60ddf_4080x3072.jpeg 1080w, https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0fecbc06-3c36-4c52-90fe-97c92ad60ddf_4080x3072.jpeg 1456w" sizes="(max-width: 60rem) 100vw, 56rem" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>

The shrine of the Pirs of Luari Sharif sits inside a fortified sanctuary. Until the 1930s the pilgrimage here was the largest annual Muslim pilgrimage after the Hajj, rivalling Mecca itself, until the colonial government put a stop to mass pilgrimage. It has been much restored, most recently after the earthquake of 2014, with bricks copied to the original composition and the painted decoration followed exactly.

Pir Saleh, my host’s younger brother and the mayor of Badin, made his devotions at the threshold, laying his head and each cheek against the doorpost. He showed me the well, the shelters, the kitchens. Behind the mosque, past the ancestral house of the Pirs – derelict, awaiting restoration, its 1920s Belgian coloured glass still in place, as Pir Saleh pointed out with proprietorial gloom – stands a long building open from floor to roof at the centre. This is the ‘generous kitchen’, where the Pirs stored grain and gave bread and rice to pilgrims without charge.

And there, reached through brambles and past architectural fragments left deliberately where they fell so that they may one day be put back, is a millstone of a distinctive pink stone. It was sent by Tipu Sultan, Tiger of Mysore, a follower of the shrine, who had wished to send the Pir a great quantity of gems and treasure. Word came back that the guardian of the shrine could not accept a gift so worldly. Tipu sent the millstone instead – to grind the flour, to bake the bread, to feed the pilgrims, and so to collect the blessings of every one of them. In all my years working with people who collect and give things to institutions, I have never encountered a shrewder piece of philanthropy.

## What is actually being built

It would be easy to write all this up as picturesque and leave it there, which would be a lie and give only a partial account. Badin’s local government has largely failed, and the vacuum is being filled by the family whose shrine I had just been shown round.

A derelict school has become an engineering institute, cleaned out and equipped with machinery found through French (Schneider Electric) and other foreign supporters, where three hundred students learn circuitry and take internal combustion engines apart and put them back together. At the Sufi Institute a troop of Scouts in pink and green scarves formed up as we arrived; they had been the local community’s most reliable resource through the pandemic. A teacher took me into a classroom where the children were learning to code – girls on one side of the aisle, boys on the other – and next door two girls addressed me in Mandarin with complete confidence and evident enjoyment. Panels along the hall set out, in plain terms, the religion, language and culture of Persia, the Arab world, Europe and Britain.

Pir’s ambition is to make Badin a centre for technical skills. His argument, which he put to a room of community leaders that morning sitting cross-legged before him, is that with education, electricity, a fast fibre optic connection (already installed, linking Badin to Karachi) and a laptop, a person can build something from anywhere, and that money spent on a child now is repaid many times over by their grandchildren. To confirm this commitment, nearby he has given away the land for the construction of a university - to be built by the Pakistan local government.

Close by, a shell of a building designed in a stylish pared-back 1930s modernist idiom will become a [museum](https://psacm.com/) to house the manuscripts I had been shown, with two bedrooms for visiting scholars, so that the collection can at last be catalogued, photographed and published - and visited by all.

## Thatta, and a ‘mere collector’

We stopped at Makli on the road back, the necropolis above Thatta, where Pir’s ancestors studied when the town was a centre of Islamic learning. We saw two spectacular Mughal tombs out of hundreds and left; two children were begging at the gate, and Pir emptied his pocket to them as we passed.

In Karachi we called on Mr Hameed Haroon, a renowned collector, who lives in the historic Civil Lines Quarter. I do not think I can adequately describe his house. A nineteenth-century French marble figure of a woman, plausibly Carrier-Belleuse, in the entrance hall. Huge Ancient Greek mythological triptychs painted by Kyriacos Katzoulakis (1944-2021) lining the staircase, one of Medea, one of Alexander at the Indus. A two-storey library with a gallery and books flowing onto the floor, in the middle of which stood a reproduction of Chippendale’s great mahogany library table at Nostell Priory, buried under papers. Then an hour’s circuit: an icon from Mount Athos, a Tibetan *thangka*, Lahore School pictures, in fact one of the prime collections of 20th century Pakistan paintings 1947-1971, an early Damien Hirst (pre-*Freeze*), a corridor of murals of imperial battles - he named it ‘the Colonial Corridor’; Jinnah’s carpet, sculpted marble figures recovered from a colonial-period waste site, and relief-carved marble panels bought at a (nameless) London auction house, whose samples an Italian laboratory matched — by crystalline signature — to marble extracted quarries destined for the Mughal Emperor Shah Jahan's great project, the Taj Mahal. Much of this was all shown beneath painted ceilings copied from those in the famous Renaissance palaces in Ferrara and Mantua.

He was very funny about all of it. The pleasure was plainly in the hunt – the waiting, the auction sat out, the dealer who did not know what he had – and he described his own parsimony with the relish of a man describing a virtue. As we left he apologised for the thinness of the tour, on the grounds that he was a mere collector.

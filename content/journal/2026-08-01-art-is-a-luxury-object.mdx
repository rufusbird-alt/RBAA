At last, we can stop the pretence that art appeals in some ethereal, unique way that is different to reproducible luxury objects. When Christie’s released a [promotional video](https://www.christies.com/en/stories/nicole-kidman-constantin-brancusi-danaide-1cd4050788274d24b590e279a4078109) of the Australian actress Nicole Kidman cavorting around Constantin Brâncuşi’s beautiful gilded bronze sculpture *Danaïde* of 1913, there were mutterings and chunterings. Here was a Hollywood star, commissioned to promote a great work of art! A great sculpture, the chunterers complained, had been reduced to the status of a bottle of expensive perfume.

The art market and auction houses have been selling handbags, fashion, sportswear, dinosaurs, Hermes ashtrays, sports cars, expensive watches for decades, and the fashion world has long collaborated with artists. The apparent offence is not only the debasement of a work of art in said video, but the egregious sin of selling these great works of art for gigantic prices. *[Danaïde](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-6585085)* sold for $107.5m, Jackson Pollock’s *[Number 7A](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-6585089)*, 1948 sold for $181m - both from the collection of S. I. Newhouse, the Conde Nast billionaire, auctioned at Christie’s in New York this May. One commentator was disgusted: art, he said, had been reduced to a casino chip, a financial instrument, a placeholder trophy destined for a free port. The auction house has stripped art bare of meaning and placed it ‘naked on the block with only a price to define it’. The auction was, apparently, a séance in which capital applauds itself while the rest of us pretend to witness culture.

But this is precisely backwards. The perfume advertisement vibe borrowed the language of art; Christie’s film was clever in acknowledging that the Brancusi and the perfume bottle occupy the same *economy of desire*. Both are beautiful things. Both are expensive. Both are coveted. The auction house was being honest about what a sculpture at that level of the market actually is: a luxury object of the highest order, available to a handful of people on earth, marketed accordingly. The pretence that something more elevated was being traded, some sort of scholarly transaction rather than a commercial one, would have been the real dishonesty.

The art market has rarely been only a scholarly endeavour. Properly, the scholarly function belongs to the compiler of the catalogue raisonné, or the museum, perhaps even the theoreticians at the university. The top end of the market is, and has always been, about commerce, luxury, and what sells. This is not a recent development. The pictures Titian painted for Philip II of Spain in the 1550s, the *poesie*, including *[Diana and Actaeon](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/titian-diana-and-actaeon)* and *[Diana and Callisto](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/titian-diana-and-callisto)* (London, National Gallery & Edinburgh, National Galleries of Scotland), were luxury commissions on a royal scale, painted to hang in private apartments for the pleasure of one man. The great collections that eventually became public museums : the [Wallace](https://www.wallacecollection.org/), the [Frick](https://www.frick.org/), the [Burrell](https://burrellcollection.com/), the [Gardner](https://www.gardnermuseum.org/), the multifarious [Rothschild](https://academic.oup.com/jhc/article-abstract/30/1/182/4584180) collections, were assembled by very rich people buying beautiful things for their houses, motivated by desire, status, and the pleasure of ownership in roughly equal measure. Nobody at the time felt obliged to dress this up as something else.

The dressing-up came later, following the invention of the institutions that inherited these collections and needed to justify – and explain – their authority over them. The museum required the artwork to be sacred in order for the curator to be its priest. The art market has no such need, and it is precisely that honesty that so discomforts the high-minded commentariat. When Christie’s puts Nicole Kidman in front of *Danaïde*, it is not lowering the work to the level of commerce. It is honest about its presentation of a desirable work of art.

Louis Vuitton understood this instinctively when it collaborated with Yayoi Kusama, one of the most significant artists alive, to produce a fashion collection, a suite of global installations, and a [hardback book by Rizzoli](https://uk.louisvuitton.com/eng-gb/products/yayoi-kusama-x-louis-vuitton-creating-infinity-nvprod4800001v/R09253) (£290) with essays by Hans-Ulrich Obrist and other curators who have organised major retrospectives of her work. The collaboration was an acknowledgement that Kusama’s art and Louis Vuitton’s bags occupy the same world: the world of beautiful, costly, coveted things.

The mock horror about the exorbitant prices, in the end, is not really about whether these artworks ‘deserve’ such elevated valuations. It is about authority; about who gets to say what art means and under what conditions it may be sold. The auction house’s great offence is to look beyond the museological and curatorial art world constraints of quasi-academic apparatus, and instead to treat a work of art as what it is : a beautiful, costly and desirable object rather than as a didactic exhibit. As it happens, the inspiration for the Kidman-*Danaïde* face-off was a [film shot in 1930 by Man Ray](https://youtu.be/QaDfTUde31w) of the Surrealist and photographer Lee Miller unveiling Brancusi’s *[Princess X](https://www.youtube.com/watch?t=163&v=QaDfTUde31w)<sup><a href="#fn-1" id="fnref-1">1</a></sup>*. The eroticism and the smile are present in both films.

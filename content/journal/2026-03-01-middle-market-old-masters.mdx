The headline version of the Old Masters market, as it appears in the press and in industry reports, is almost always wrong. This is not because the data is fabricated, but because the data selected for headlines — record prices at <SmallCaps>Christie's</SmallCaps> and <SmallCaps>Sotheby's</SmallCaps>, <SmallCaps>Raphael</SmallCaps> attributions and <SmallCaps>Rembrandt</SmallCaps> discoveries — represents a tiny fraction of the actual market, and a fraction that follows different laws from the rest of it.

The middle market — broadly, works between €5,000 and €500,000 at auction, which is where most serious collecting actually happens — has been doing something the headlines miss. It has been quiet, but it has not been dying.

## What the figures show

The last five major-house seasons have produced sell-through rates for Old Masters in the middle bracket that hover between 68% and 74%. These are not exceptional figures — they are, in fact, roughly in line with the twenty-year average. The works that are failing to sell are, predominantly, works that are poorly documented, heavily restored, or estimated above where the market currently sits. The works that are selling well are the same ones that always sell well: works with clear attribution, good provenance, interesting subject matter, and estimates that reflect current demand rather than sellers' hopes.

## The misread narrative

The narrative of collapse originates in two places. The first is the genuinely difficult position of the top of the market: works valued above €5 million in the Old Masters category have faced real headwinds over the last decade, as new money has tended toward contemporary art and the collector base for works at that level has not expanded. This is real. But it is a problem for a very small number of works, and it does not describe the experience of the collector who is building a collection of seventeenth-century Dutch cabinet pictures or eighteenth-century Italian drawings.

The second source of the narrative is the decline of the dedicated Old Masters sale. Many houses have folded their dedicated sessions into broader sales or merged them with other European paintings periods. This looks like decline. It is actually a rationalisation of a market that was over-divided: a single strong evening sale with well-chosen works routinely outperforms a poorly curated dedicated session.

## What this means for collectors

The practical implication is counterintuitive: the middle market is, in several respects, a better environment for serious collectors than it has been for twenty years. Works are more attainable. Estimates, after a decade of overcorrection at the top, are more realistic. The competition for strong attributable works remains real — good things are always contested — but the noise level has dropped, and with it some of the speculation that drove prices beyond what the fundamentals supported.

The collector who is building a focused collection now, in a market that most commentators describe as difficult, is doing exactly what the most successful collectors of the 1970s and 1980s did in the conditions that then obtained. The conditions were not easy. Neither were the results ordinary.

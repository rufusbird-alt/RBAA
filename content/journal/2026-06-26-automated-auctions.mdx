Christie’s new auctioneers’ rostrum, designed by Sir Jony Ive and Marc Newsom’s collective Lovefrom, can possibly be seen as a statement of intent from the international art business where in-person live auctioneering is at the core of its brand identity and regular operations. Although online auctions are becoming increasingly widespread, high-value sales still seem to require a human agent overseeing the auction process.

Consider the rostrum for a moment: the previous example was made in mahogany, copying what may have been James Christie’s 18th century rostrum, said to have been designed by Thomas Chippendale.

The new rostrum is made of oak - a timber identifiably English, but also a humble choice, which gives off something of an egalitarian vibe. Had it been made in mahogany, rosewood - or trendy wengewood, it would reek of grandeur or the esoteric. Perhaps this is intentional. The sleek, aerodynamic lines give the blocky piece of furniture a sense of lightness, suggesting that it might even have been intended to take off and fly through the air. In fact, as it is made from thick oak planks, it is sturdy and very heavy. The essential 18th century design of the original is detectable in the new version. The raised counter is there - on which the auctioneer places the book and beside which the gavel is struck to signal the conclusion of a sale. The incised rounded panels on the front and sides are a faint echo of its predecessor, and the rounded off front panel extends down to rounded off legs. This being the 21st century, and being a focal point of an international business, there are no frivolities: all obvious decorations are eschewed for the sake of projecting a serious face. Adrien Meyer of Christie’s, one of the most assured and brilliant auctioneers anywhere in the world today says “I love it. I find it elegant, polished, stylish and could imagine it well going on to be used by generations to come, ... it “feels” great which proves its brilliance as a design”.

I trained as an auctioneer at Christie’s, took sales at Christie’s in South Kensington saleroom and at King Street so I was intrigued by this renewal : if the rostrum was a car it might be rather like ditching the Bentley and replacing it with a McLaren

In the art world, auctions are necessary, deeply traditional and usually like watching paint dry.  It wasn’t always like this: when Getty and Norton Simon were buying in the post-war era, there was certainly a human drama which played out, theatrically, in the saleroom for everyone to see. I remember Charlie Hindlip, Chairman of Christie’s, all of sudden switching into perfect French mid-auction, apparently for the benefit of the telephone bidder. Before he died Charlie told me that the great collector Stavros Niarchos insisted on communicating his bids via telephone to Charlie, which was complicated by the fact that he was also the auctioneer. As this was pre-mobile telephone days, a handset was left on the counter through which Charlie spoke while he auctioned the lot on which Niarchos was bidding.

Is auctioneering another human skill destined for the automation chopping block? Already timed online auctions - successfully mastered by eBay years ago - have, rather found their way into the traditional world of the gavel-wielding auctioneer. But how will the sharp-suited, couture-clad, smooth-syllabled auctioneer maintain their pre-eminence ? Since the arrival of international jet travel in the 1950s, increasingly collectors were prepared to fly across continents to attend sales and return home within days. However, technology has a double-edge, and while some of the international jet-set were delighted to show their faces to bid on a Braque at Sotheby’s, more and more were (and are) coy and opted for secrecy using the telephone to conduct their bidding. The technological innovation of the internet has added another layer of secrecy: no need to rely on your phone signal and a human to relay your bid - only the speed of your internet connection will hold back an online bid.

Such technological innovations have also made the job of the auctioneer harder: how to keep the watching punters interested ? And make no mistake, the majority of those sitting in the room are watching. Yes, there are a handful who make the effort to come to the room and bid and buy. The so-called ‘action’ takes place behind the telephone.

And so the auctioneer is relegated to a mediator between his or her colleagues on the telephones, occasionally sharing a joke as if no one else is watching. And where the auctioneer used to cajole and charm, pause and speed up, today the auction house staffer has to play that role. Adrien Meyer says that he views his colleagues on the telephone “as my most important clients, and I want them to feel that way during an auction as they represent the end buyer on the phone. They often feel the client’s excitement and convey it to the room which becomes entertaining.”  If fine art auctions are actually theatrical and exciting, then there is a chance they will remain conducted by people - if not, in person auctioneering is surely another area where machines will take over.

*Amid the back and forth of debates about the direction of the National Trust and select committee [drama](https://parliamentlive.tv/event/index/027ca9ff-b3e9-4e7d-bebe-cdb5443317b4), a serene walk through Mottisfont makes the Trust’s strongest argument.* The National Trust (NT) has suffered increasing criticism over the past decade for many reasons, including handling the re-presentation of Clandon Park following the devastating fire in 2015 ([here](https://www.restoretrust.org.uk/media-and-press/ten-years-after-a-catastrophic-fire-clandon-park-remains-a-ruin-and-a-potent-symbol-of-the-national-trusts-muddled-priorities); the NT’s proposals can be viewed [here](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/surrey/clandon-park/the-project-at-clandon-park)) and its ‘dumbed down’ presentation for children at [Sudbury](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/peak-district-derbyshire/the-childrens-country-house-at-sudbury). I attended the 2023 National Trust AGM in Swindon, with much passionate discussion about what the NT should or shouldn’t be doing.  Therefore, I came to Mottisfont - as it is called by the NT - with an open mind. It was my first visit.

Even before my 16th birthday when I was presented with life membership of the National Trust by my wonderful Berliner godparents I have loved visiting old houses and collections.<sup><a href="#fn-1" id="fnref-1">1</a></sup> My membership card has been very well used in the intervening thirty-five years. Much to my children’s bemusement, I take every opportunity to visit old houses, churches and other ancient sites, infrequently - happily for them - dragging them along too.

First impressions. It was busy. The car park was large and was almost entirely full. Perhaps not surprising as it was August and a pleasant summer’s day.  I parked and walked to the, very large rather agricultural barn-like visitor centre. Whilst not perfect, this is a well-designed, functional and rather pleasing building, made of wood and steel, with pierced metal which had rusted and weathered (as intended) and gave the building a sort of rustic-functional feel. There are agricultural-barn references blended with an appreciation of nature. Disappointingly - and rather curiously - the NT team thought it necessary to place outside the entrance one of those brightly-coloured bendy flags which can be seen at temporary sporting events or car shows. It spoiled the look of this sharp, but not inelegant, admissions centre, which had obviously been designed with considerable care.

The team behind the desk were delightful and handed me a map. They pointed out the house and garden and sent me on my way. Over the bridge, below which a branch of the river Test bubbled, and a just past the chestnut trees, a glimpse of the south front of Mottisfont Abbey was discernible.

There were families of all ages everywhere picnicking, little NT buggies (presumably to transport less able visitors) glided up and down the gravel pathways, it all seemed rather delightful. I decided to walk around the house before penetrating its darkened rooms. To the east - between the chalk stream and the house - white-painted metal tables and chairs were set out where lunch and tea were served from the small cafe inside. Children ran up and down the path by the stream, but the swans remained unperturbed.

I chose a spot near the south front to read the modern guidebook. As part of the evolution of the Trust in recent years, much has been made of the loss of information between today’s and earlier editions of country house guidebooks produced by the National Trust. I brought my copy of James Lees-Milne’s guide (revised edition) of 1978, based on his original text of 1967 ‘with only a few revisions and additions’.  While James Lees-Milne’s writing is hard to improve upon :

> ‘Today the Abbey grounds present an aspect of immemorial beauty. The ancient house lies low on green lawns adorned with enormous plane, cedar, chestnut and beech trees.’…,

I found the present-day equivalent to be clear, comprehensive and broader in its scope than its earlier counterpart. Rather than a ‘tour of the house’, the guide covered 800 years of its history:

> ‘First came the hospitable Augustinians who received pilgrims eager to be blessed at the priory, where the forefinger of St John the Baptist was held as a sacred relic and where Peter de Rivallis (d. 1262), the nephew of the Bishop of Winchester was buried deep within the walls.’

Walking around to the entrance just to the west of the south front, on the higher ground away from the stream, I entered the house: into a rather bland room and was greeted by a characteristically enthusiastic NT volunteer, who offered me some sort of house guide sheet - a themed ‘invitation’. It didn’t seem to make much sense, although I could see what the NT team were trying to do.

The walls of the circulation spaces (staircases and corridors) were rather magnificently painted to imitate Siena marble in the early 20th century, and this has been retained. The corridor connecting the principal rooms is also furnished with Regency furniture - an interiors style sometimes called ‘Vogue Regency’ made fashionable in the early 20th century by the likes of Ralph Dutton (1898-1985) at [Hinton Ampner](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/hampshire/hinton-ampner/the-house-at-hinton-ampner), Lord Gerald Wellesley (1885-1972) at [Stratfield Saye](https://wellington.co.uk/stratfield-saye-house/) (both houses in Hampshire) and more purist collectors of Regency furniture such as Edward Knoblock (1874-1945) and his collection of Thomas Hope furniture at the Beach House in Worthing (see [here](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O78951/armchair-unknown/) and [here](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O11752/the-denon-chair-armchair-denon-dominique-vivant/)).

As the house contents - with the exception of the foregoing - are rather lacking in quality and interest, the Mottisfont team have tried, valiantly, to generate some other interest in the house and its connections.  On my visit I was directed to the first and second floors to be shown a series of rooms with large, text heavy, graphic panels explaining the friendship between the Russells of Mottisfont and Ian Fleming, author and creator of James Bond. Here was a rather jazzy exhibition on his final book, Chitty Chitty Bang Bang, written for his son Jasper. This was something of a trip down memory lane for me, as I remember as a child reading the book, with its brilliant illustrations by John Burningham, and not long after, listening to the audio tape on family car journeys - a quite brilliant version narrated by Lionel Jefferies, which entered into family legend (and suffered numerous retellings, as my siblings and I imitated the magical voice of Monsieur Bon Bon, the sweet-shop owner, as conjured up by Lionel Jefferies).

I got back into my car and drove away from Mottisfont Abbey (to visit Romsey Abbey nearby) cogitating on *the problem with the National Trust*. I enjoyed my visit and left with the impression that, in spite of the furore over a handful of the houses in its care (and this piece not the place to debate that), the National Trust presented Mottisfont Abbey well, all things considered. For all its lack of furnishings, the house and estate satisfies on many levels. First, the house is presented as best it can be, the contents having been sold off in 1932; a few pieces have returned in recent years, but to furnish the interior, the Trust is reliant on donations (such as the Derek Hill collection), loans or facsimiles. It has ventured into facsimile territory with the set of dining chairs (although I suspect it may have been easier and cheaper to buy a set rather than having a set made, copied from an old photo). The ‘Whistler Room’ was in a state of partial undress, as the curtains - as designed by Rex Whistler - were part-way through a restoration project. Rather than remove the curtains to store, the Trust has left them in the room crated up. The crates are fitted with a small inspection panel permitting a view inside of rolled up curtains (not particularly enlightening, other than to verify the contents). Hopefully funding for the restoration of these fragile textiles will be found soon.  Second, the invitation idea (a laminated card, printed with the sequence of rooms on the visitor route) fell rather flat, but at least it gave the room layout a little more ‘interest’. Nonetheless, I found it a little confusing as the house was plainly *not* set up for a party (crated curtains in the Saloon?!).  Third, the ‘summer exhibition’ idea is all very well, and gives visitors something else to look at and come away from Mottisfont with another aspect of the house’s history, but I felt that the Chitty Chitty Bang Bang subject matter was only lightly connected to the house proper, and that the subject was chosen principally to entertain children (although this adult enjoyed seeing the brilliant illustrations). Stranger still, there was an attempt to ‘keep children interested’ by putting a small toy car (Chitty?) in each room. This is a personal bugbear of mine and suggests either a lack of imagination on the part of the local team, or a fear of understanding the collections.<sup><a href="#fn-2" id="fnref-2">2</a></sup>

Perhaps previous shows have concentrated more on the varied and interesting historic occupants of the house. Here I could find no references to the slave trade, no rainbow lanyards to be seen, the text panels were succinct and, for the most part (downstairs at least) quite discreet.<sup><a href="#fn-3" id="fnref-3">3</a></sup> However, the grounds and the setting, it must be said, remain the chief attraction, and they are idyllic. The car park was full, the families were content, the cafe in the stable block was doing a roaring trade, and the house was being visited.

Clearly this is what people want to come and see: a beautiful country house in a wonderful setting with  interesting and historic connections to ponder, so long as there is time after the picnic and the children don’t kick up too much of a fuss. The Trust has been doing this brilliantly for years, and I hope it continues to focus on the essential qualities of these marvellous places - qualities in evidence at Mottisfont - : long building and family histories, drawing out their connections with people, places and objects, and providing a delightful visit in remarkable surroundings, both natural and built. For more images - see my instagram [@birdrufus](https://www.instagram.com/birdrufus/).

Prior to my visit, I looked up Mottisfont Abbey in Simon Jenkins’ book on the Thousand Best Houses in England from 2003 - who wrote, rather tartly

> “Mottisfont is a pleasant walk by wooded streams to a nationally celebrated rose garden. The house exterior is charming, but the interiors are disappointing except for one room … The Rex Whistler room, however, must not be missed”<sup><a href="#fn-4" id="fnref-4">4</a></sup>

I preferred John Julius Norwich’s write up, from his book on the architecture of Southern England of 1985:

> “This is a part of my youth; and of all the country houses of England it is, I think, the one I love the best. It could not be called classically beautiful - no building that began as a monastic foundation in the twelfth century and was subsequently converted, first into a Tudor Mansion and later into a Georgian one, could aspire to be that, but even leaving aside its considerable architectural interest, it radiates a quite extraordinary atmosphere of peace, and of mellow, timeless serenity. Many is the sunlit summer afternoon, with the tea table set out on the east lawn under one of those magnificent trees beside the swiftly flowing Test, on which I have reflected, helping myself to another cucumber sandwich, that at Mottisfont I could happily live and die, and that earth, almost certainly, had not anything to show more fair.”<sup><a href="#fn-5" id="fnref-5">5</a></sup>

The first five years of serious collecting are the years when most of the permanent mistakes are made. This is not a reflection on the collector's intelligence or taste — it is a structural observation about the learning curve, and about the peculiar way that the art market provides feedback.

In other markets, a bad decision becomes visible quickly. In art, a bad decision can sit undetected on a wall for decades. The work looks as good at year fifteen as it did at year one. The price at which you could sell it may have moved in either direction. The work's place in your collection — its coherence with what you have since acquired, its relevance to where your taste has gone — changes slowly and is only visible at a distance.

## The first year

The first year is characterised by enthusiasm and relative safety. Enthusiasm, because almost everything in the world of serious art is new and interesting. Relative safety, because in the first year a collector is not yet certain enough of their own taste to overpay for the wrong things. The purchases tend to be cautious. Some of them are excellent.

The danger in the first year is not overpaying. It is buying too broadly — acquiring a wide range of works across different periods, subjects, and media — before the taste has had time to crystallise. A collection that begins with two Dutch landscapes, a Georgian watercolour, a Regency piece of furniture, and a piece of Art Nouveau silver is a collection that has not yet found itself. This is not shameful; it is normal. But it means that in year three or four, when the taste does crystallise, several of those early purchases will feel like early mistakes.

## The third year: why it is dangerous

The third year is dangerous for a specific reason: the collector has learned enough to be confident, but not enough to be reliably right. The vocabulary is in place — attribution, condition, provenance, comparative sales — but the judgement that translates vocabulary into correct decisions takes longer to develop. The third-year collector knows what questions to ask. They do not yet always know what to do with the answers.

The result is a particular kind of purchase: the ambitious acquisition, bought with conviction, at the top of the market for that particular work. In the Old Masters field, this often means a painting by a name that carries prestige — a follower of <SmallCaps>Rubens</SmallCaps>, a circle of <SmallCaps>Van Dyck</SmallCaps> — at a price that reflects the name rather than the quality of the specific work.

## What to wait for

The single most useful piece of advice I give to collectors beginning in their first five years is this: wait for the thing that makes you slightly nervous. The purchase that feels safe — the well-attributed work, in good condition, well within budget — is often the right purchase. But the purchase that makes you slightly nervous, because it is more expensive than you expected to spend at this stage, because it is more particular or more challenging than your other acquisitions, because it represents a commitment — that purchase, when the nervousness is grounded in genuine quality rather than mere ambition, is often where the collection begins.

The nervousness is useful. It means the work matters. The collections that I admire most were assembled by people who allowed themselves to be changed by the things they acquired, and who were willing to feel, periodically, that they had gone slightly further than they were quite comfortable going.

A valuation is not a price. This is the sentence that needs to be said clearly, early, and often, because the conflation of the two is the source of a great many disputes, disappointments, and professional misunderstandings.

There are at least four distinct types of valuation in regular use, and they are different enough that the same work — the same canvas, the same artist, the same condition — can legitimately generate four different figures, none of which is wrong. The difference lies in the question being asked, and the question being asked depends entirely on the purpose to which the valuation will be put.

## The four valuations

**Fair market value** is defined as the price at which a work would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither under any compulsion to buy or sell and both having reasonable knowledge of the relevant facts. This is the valuation used for donation, gift tax, and most estate planning. It does not reflect what you paid for the work, what you could get for it at auction next month, or what your insurer will pay you if it is destroyed.

**Insurance replacement value** is almost always higher than fair market value — sometimes significantly so. It reflects the cost of replacing the work with something of comparable quality and importance in the retail market, including dealer's commission and any premium for urgency. It also, in principle, accounts for the fact that you may not be able to find an exact equivalent and may need to pay a premium for the closest available substitute.

**Probate value** is the value for inheritance tax purposes. In the <SmallCaps>UK</SmallCaps>, this is typically fair market value as determined at the date of death, but the practical application involves judgements about the state of the market, the specific auction record for the artist, and whether the estate is likely to sell the work or retain it. HMRC has its own view on these questions, and it is not always the same as the valuator's.

**Retail replacement** is what a dealer would charge you for a comparable work. It is almost always the highest of the four figures and is sometimes used for insurance, though it needs to be distinguished carefully from auction-based fair market value.

## Why the four almost never agree

The gap between fair market value and insurance replacement value is the one that surprises people most. A work valued at £80,000 for estate purposes might carry an insurance replacement value of £120,000 or more. Both figures are professionally supportable. The reason is simple: fair market value reflects a transaction between two informed parties at arm's length; insurance replacement value reflects the cost of solving a problem at short notice in a retail environment.

This matters most when a claim is made. If your policy covers fair market value and you believed you were covered for replacement cost, the gap between the two figures becomes very real at exactly the moment when you are least equipped to deal with it.

## What follows from this

The practical implication is that different valuations are needed for different purposes, and that a single document — however well prepared — cannot serve all four. Estate planning, insurance scheduling, family distribution, and eventual sale each require their own assessment, and the assessments should be refreshed periodically as the market moves.

This is not a reason to be alarmed. It is a reason to be clear about what you are asking for, and why.

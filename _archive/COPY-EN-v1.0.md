### 2.2 — The manifesto

Piers's "Why Collecting Requires Counsel" piece, in full. Six short paragraphs. [P, verbatim with light cuts]

Collecting is not a transaction. Transactions are rational. Collecting is not.

Art is one of the few assets whose value is inseparable from personal meaning. Two collectors can look at the same work and see entirely different worth. What compels one person leaves another indifferent. Price follows taste as much as the other way around.

This makes collecting fundamentally different from other forms of acquisition. It is shaped by identity, ambition, reputation, emotion, and legacy — not simply market mechanics.

Access to art can be learned. Markets can be navigated. *What cannot be systematised is judgement.*

True collecting requires someone who can sit inside the client's world — understanding not only what is available, but what is right for them. Someone who can challenge impulse, sharpen instinct, and translate unspoken ambition into coherent direction over time.

Without counsel, collections become accidental. *With counsel, they become intentional.*

The role of a genuine advisor is not to facilitate transactions, but to help a collector think clearly about what they are building, why it matters, and how it should evolve. The outcome is not simply ownership of objects, but the construction of meaning, coherence, and personal legacy.

*This is the difference between buying art and building a collection.*

Every engagement begins with a conversation. ### 3.3 — `/services/family-office`

**Page title:** The Family Office — Stewarding Significant Assets. [S, following Zing template]

Family offices exist to hold the long view. Art, furniture, silver, books and the rest of what a family has accumulated over generations sit awkwardly in that view — valuable, illiquid, often under-documented, and frequently the subject of disagreement between family members who have inherited it without the context that originally gave it meaning.

You may be facing any of the recurring challenges: an inheritance that needs valuing for probate; a decision about which pieces to hold and which to release; a collection spread across several residences that has never been fully catalogued; or a next-generation conversation about what the collection is *for*.

Rufus works alongside family office principals, trustees, and legal counsel to bring order and clarity to these questions. He provides defensible valuations for tax and insurance, full room-by-room catalogues that become the authoritative record, sale strategies that maximise dignity as well as price, and — where it is wanted — a long view on how the collection might evolve for the next generation. Every engagement is unconflicted, carried out for a transparent fee, with no commissions taken from either side of any transaction.

The outcome is a collection that is no longer a source of administrative weight, but an asset the family understands, documents, and — where appropriate — enjoys.

*A steward's work is invisible when it is done well. The collection simply becomes easier to hold.*

The rooms are coming together. The architecture, the finishes, the furniture: all of these are your work, and the eye that pulled them together. What remains is the art — and the art must do more than fill a wall. It must hold the room. Become its centre. Lift everything around it.

### 3.5 — `/services/estate-trustee`

**Page title:** The Estate Trustee — Understanding What You Hold. [S, following Zing template]

A great house, an inherited collection, a palazzo in Venice or a manoir in Normandy. The trustees — often new to the assets, sometimes newly appointed — are asked to make decisions about art, furniture, silver and archives that they have rarely had time to examine. Beneficiaries are waiting. Insurers are waiting. The tax authorities are waiting.

What is needed first is not a sale strategy. It is simply to know what is there.

Rufus begins where no one else does: he walks the building. Room by room. He catalogues what is present and reconstructs the provenance of what has moved. He tests attributions that have been inherited with the property, many of which have not been looked at in a generation. He values for probate, for insurance, for family distribution, and for eventual sale — separately, because the four rarely agree. He coordinates storage, conservation, real-estate liaison, and the quiet, discreet sales that are sometimes the right answer.

He has done this for trustees of a Venetian palazzo, and for a number of other estates he does not name. In every case, the work is undertaken at an agreed fee, with full disclosure, and with a willingness to be there for as long as the estate continues to need counsel.

*The first job is to know what is there. Every decision improves after that.*

### 4.2 — `/case-studies/venetian-palazzo`

**Page title:** A Venetian Palazzo, Inherited in Full. [S synthesising discussion notes]

**Hero image** — a full-bleed interior, placeholder for v1.

**Caption / metadata block:**
- Mandate: Full-collection advisory and dispersal
- Object types: Paintings, furniture, *objets*, archives
- Location: Venice
- Client: Trustees of a historic estate (confidential)
- Scope: Catalogue · valuation · storage · sale strategy · real-estate liaison
- Duration: 2024–ongoing, on retainer

**Body** (following the Object → Mandate → Investigation → Judgement → Outcome arc):

A palazzo on the Grand Canal, four hundred years in one family, came to trustees who needed to understand what they had before they could decide what to keep, what to sell, and what to hold for the beneficiaries. The collection was the building as much as the objects in it: portraits hung where they had been commissioned, furniture in the rooms it had been made for, correspondence in the drawers it had been written at.

The mandate was the whole thing. Catalogue the collection room by room. Reconstruct provenance where it had been lost. Value for probate and for eventual sale. Organise off-site storage for what was to be held. Liaise with the real-estate agency handling the building itself. And, quietly, identify anything that had been mistaken for ordinary.

Two discoveries mattered most. The first was structural: a significant group of drawings in a trunk in the attic, uncatalogued in any surviving inventory, that expanded the picture of the family's patronage in the seventeenth century. The second was smaller but reads well: a Vietnamese bronze in the side of a cabinet, catalogued for a century as *"oriental, nineteenth century."* It was neither. It was early sixteenth, and the second of its kind to surface outside museum collections in the last forty years.

The work took two years and is continuing. The trustees are on retainer. I have my own set of keys.

*The first job is always the same: know what is there. Everything improves after that.*

## Tone rules (for any copy added after this document)

1. **Simple, emotional, refined, bespoke.** [Z brand descriptors]
2. **Personable warmth over aloof professionalism.** [Z]
3. **Storytelling is key.** Every sentence should either tell a story, sharpen a distinction, or clear something up. Nothing should merely fill the page. [Z]
4. **No marketing verbs.** Banned list: *leverage, deliver, unlock, elevate, curated, bespoke (as a lazy modifier), tailored, reach out, solution, offering.*
5. **First person singular, not plural,** unless specifically describing an engagement with named specialists. The practice is one principal; the site should say so.
6. **Concrete over abstract.** "A North Italian panel, Cremonese school, c. 1560" beats "a distinguished Old Master picture."
7. **Footnotes are real, numbered, and linked.** Especially in journal entries. Cheapest signal of seriousness available.
8. **Italics for emphasis, not bold.** The display face carries emphasis better in italic. Bold exists but is reserved.
9. **Small caps** for artist and place names in running text. Signals expertise without italicising.
10. **Quiet punctuation.** Em-dash, not double-hyphen. Single spaces after full stops. Oxford comma used.
